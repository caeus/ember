# The Cat with the Bell

By the time the second plate of chips arrived, Lena had three conversations open on her phone and a very definite opinion about each of them.

"The teacher asks questions," she said. "Real questions. The one with the motorcycle just sends photographs of his lunch."

"And the third?"

Lena turned the screen toward Mara. A woman with close-cropped hair was asking whether Lena wanted to go dancing on Friday.

"Oh, I like her," Mara said.

"You're not supposed to choose for me."

"You asked."

"I asked you to look."

They had been having variations of this argument for eleven years. Mara took a chip, dragged it through the last of the mayonnaise and let Lena make up her own mind.

The breakup with Daniel had been six weeks ago. Lena had spent the first two sleeping badly and calling him, the next two insisting she was perfectly fine, and the last two going out in clothes that still had tags attached. Tonight she was wearing a red shirt she'd bought that afternoon. The label had left a little white thread at the collar.

"I'd forgotten what it's like," Lena said, putting the phone face down. It lit up against the table. "People wanting to know things about you."

Mara watched her turn it over again.

She had finished one beer and was halfway through a glass of sparkling water. Lena was ordering a third wine. Mara could have objected, but they both knew how the evening would end: she'd get Lena home, find her something to drink that wasn't alcohol, and make sure she didn't message Daniel at two in the morning.

"You're doing it," Lena said.

"Doing what?"

"Leaving. With your eyes."

Mara hadn't noticed she was looking at the door.

"Work?"

"Nothing."

"That's convincing."

The woman from Tuesday came back to her. She had called the police because she thought someone had been inside her house. All the kitchen drawers were open and the cutlery had been arranged on the floor in neat rows. Nothing was missing. The woman was frightened, and her husband had been furious that the officers wouldn't treat it as a burglary.

A neighbour's door camera showed the husband carrying the cutlery out to the landing and sorting it there, one handful at a time. He was barefoot and wearing the shirt he wore to bed. Twice he had stepped aside for someone coming up the stairs. On the recording he looked awake enough to be embarrassed.

He said he remembered going to sleep. He remembered breakfast. He remembered nothing between.

"You know how someone can drive somewhere and barely remember the journey?" Mara said.

"I do that all the time."

"Not like that."

Lena listened as Mara told her about the husband. Then she considered it with an earnestness the wine had made slightly exaggerated.

"Sleepwalking," she said.

"Probably."

"People do things in their sleep."

"Yes."

"So?"

Mara couldn't explain what was bothering her without sounding foolish. It wasn't that people did strange things. Police work was mostly a record of people doing strange things. It was how well the man had behaved while doing them. He had waited for a neighbour to pass. He had moved the knives out of reach of a child. He had appeared, in every ordinary way, to know what he was doing.

And there had been the shop assistant who had worked most of a shift and later couldn't account for four hours of it. He knew which customer had complained, and why. He remembered the password for the till. He had called the police only because he found his car where he was certain he hadn't parked it.

Her sergeant had suggested stress. They'd written it down. It was as good a word as any.

Lena touched her wrist.

"You're off tonight."

"I know."

"Then be off. Look at this woman's arms."

Mara looked. Lena laughed, pleased with herself, and ordered dessert.

By eleven, she had decided that dancing on Friday was an excellent idea, that the motorcycle was not, and that she still loved Daniel but no longer wanted him in her kitchen. She cried a little when she said the last part. Mara waited until she was finished, then passed her a napkin.

They took a taxi. Lena spent most of the ride trying to enter a reply on her phone and deleting it, dissatisfied with every version. Mara took the phone from her at the building entrance, slipped it into Lena's coat pocket, and paid the driver.

The apartment smelled faintly of oranges and old washing. There were shoes all over the hallway. Lena dropped her keys twice before getting the door closed.

A grey cat came out of the kitchen as they entered. It had a white chin and a small bell on a blue collar. The bell gave a brief, clear sound when it rubbed against the leg of the hall table.

"Mind your feet," Lena said, pushing a shoe aside.

Mara stepped around the cat and hung up their coats. It followed them into the sitting room, where a laundry basket had been serving as a coffee table for some time. Lena sat on the edge of the sofa and began looking for her phone.

"It's in your pocket."

"No, I had it in the taxi."

"You had it at the door."

"I need it. I was talking to someone."

"Several people."

Lena checked the wrong pocket, stood up too quickly, and sat down again. In the confusion her phone slipped out of her coat and disappeared behind the sofa.

Mara heard the bell first. The cat was crouched beside the sofa, patting at something in the gap beneath it. A pale rectangle lit up against the skirting board. She reached down and retrieved the phone.

"See?" she said. "An excellent assistant."

"Good boy," Lena murmured, already reading a message.

Mara got a glass of water from the kitchen, put it on the bedside table, and found a clean T-shirt in a pile of laundry. Lena was sitting on the closed toilet lid, trying to take off an earring. Mara held out her hand for it, then for the other one.

"Remember when you cut my hair?" Lena asked.

"You asked me to."

"I asked you to trim it."

"You moved."

"I was sitting perfectly still."

"You were laughing."

They both were now. Lena brushed her teeth with one hand against the sink to steady herself. In the bedroom, Mara persuaded her to remove the red shirt before sleeping in it. The cat had settled on the folded duvet. Lena nudged it aside with the back of her hand and climbed into bed.

"Friday," Lena said.

"What about it?"

"Don't let me cancel."

"I won't."

"If I say it's because I'm tired, I'm lying."

"I know."

Lena was asleep before Mara had switched off the light.

Mara went back to the sitting room and answered one message from her sergeant. Nothing urgent. She set an alarm, folded her jacket under her head and lay down on the sofa. The cat climbed onto the other end, turned around twice, and settled against her calves. Its bell touched the wooden frame with a faint tick.

She was thinking of the man on the landing again, moving the knives out of reach of a child. At some point the thought stopped.

In the morning, Lena was already in the kitchen, holding a mug with both hands and looking sorry for herself.

"Did I cry?"

"Briefly."

"Did I text Daniel?"

"No."

"Then I consider it a success."

Mara found two tablets for Lena's headache and started gathering the glasses. Her neck hurt from sleeping on the sofa. The apartment was bright enough to show dust on the shelves and crumbs beneath the table.

She looked for the cat. It wasn't on the sofa or in the kitchen. There was no food dish by the refrigerator, no water bowl under the table. She couldn't remember seeing either one the night before.

"How long have you had the cat?" she asked.

Lena lowered her mug.

"What cat?"

"The grey one. Little bell on its collar."

Lena stared at her for a moment.

"Mara, I don't have a cat."

Mara opened her mouth to remind her that she'd said *good boy* when the cat found her phone. Then she remembered that Lena hadn't looked up from the screen.

She had also told Mara to mind her feet.

There had been shoes all over the hallway.
