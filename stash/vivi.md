I met Vivian because, well, she’s my wife’s best friend.

Before we met—back when I was still dating my wife—Vivian wasn’t exactly a fan of our relationship. She tried to paint it as something ephemeral, something mundane, nothing compared to the depth and permanence of real friendship. My wife, stubborn as she is, never fell for it. In fact, I sometimes wonder if one of the reasons my wife stayed with me was simply to prove Vivian wrong.

But then I met Vivian in person, and something unexpected happened: we clicked. Instantly. The rapport between us was so strong that my wife even warned me about it. She said it could come across as flirtatious. And I’ll admit, I felt drawn to Vivian. Not in a way that threatened my marriage, but in the way I’m always drawn to interesting people—and Vivian was undeniably interesting.

That’s saying something, because I don’t even find Vivian attractive. Not that she’s ugly, but she’s not my type. She’s tomboyish, not particularly feminine, and those traits are tightly woven into her identity. She sees herself as someone above the vain, shallow pursuits of beauty and style. She’s proud of it, really.

Around the time we met, she had just come out as bisexual and was dipping her toes into the dating world as a lesbian. I empathized with her almost instantly. I saw myself in her grandiose, academically leaning ambitions—the kind I used to have—and in her dating struggles, which also felt familiar. I wanted to see her succeed. I felt this strong, almost compulsive need to help her navigate her obstacles.

But every attempt I made to help her left me frustrated. Deeply, intensely frustrated.

Vivian is, without a doubt, one of the most reliable people I’ve ever met—but only when it comes to others. When it comes to herself, she’s lost. Her vision of the world is rigid, hardened by the traits she’s assigned herself: the girl who’s unburdened by the mundane, the girl who’s above it all. But that very rigidity is what holds her back.

Take her dating life, for example. It’s a mess, and it’s not hard to see why. She refuses to play by the rules that govern human social interactions. Every exchange, every communication comes with a subtle exchange of value, power, attraction—it’s nuanced, and it’s difficult to grasp. But because she’s above the “shallow and mundane,” she refuses to see how her approach is sabotaging her. She thinks she can just declare unearned love and commitment, bypassing all the natural, unspoken dynamics that build connection.

I tried to help her. I told her how her intensity could push people away, how her refusal to engage with the subtle dance of dating was isolating her. But she wouldn’t listen. Instead, she’d retreat into long, self-referential, circular explanations about why her way was better. Why she was above it all. Why she didn’t need to change.

Those conversations always ended the same way: with frustration. With sadness. With the sinking realization that she couldn’t—or wouldn’t—see what was holding her back.

Her grandiose ambitions? They’ve never come to fruition. Not her world-changing ideas, not her niche job offers, not her entrepreneurial projects, not her romantic affairs, not her goals, not her dreams. Nothing.

Nothing.

It took me a little over a year to start detaching myself from her well-being. I realized my quest to help her was a fruitless bet, one that would only bring me heartbreak.

I can’t say I don’t care for her. I can’t say I don’t appreciate her or enjoy her presence in my life. But I’ve stopped trying to help her. I’ve stopped because I know she doesn’t have the strength to confront her own shortcomings. For someone who claims to be above the shallowness of life, she’s always caught in the shallowness of her own failures. She proudly accepts the tip of the iceberg, yet never dares to acknowledge the humongous monstrosity that lurkes below the surface.

And so, I’ve let go. Not of her, but of the idea that I can save her.
