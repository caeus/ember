Hugo drags the crate inside, kicks the door shut with his heel, and laughs because the units look like nothing. Four palm-sized hypercore bricks, scuffed, still smelling of damp lab storage. He drops them under his desk, wires them to a dumb power strip, and leaves them humming like forgotten chargers. They’re running a half-baked crypto experiment he sketched in a notebook—hashing nonsense for a vanity chain no one asked for—mostly to see if the PHCUs handle random workloads without melting.

Victoria shows up that night, eyes hungry, fingers already at his belt. They barely make it to the couch. The units sit in the corner, lifeless in appearance. During, there’s nothing but sweat and the heat of her skin; the world is narrow. Afterward, she lies on his chest, tracing circles on his skin. "You taste like coffee and stale energy drinks," she says. He teases, "does your boyfriend get jealous of my screen time?" She smirks, dodges the question—he already knows who the guy is—and changes the subject by biting his shoulder. In the afterglow, with their breaths slowing, the room starts to hum—a pressure in the skull, a glow behind the eyes. It only arrives once the emotion drains out of him. He blames it on her perfume and the beer.

Days fold into each other. Hugo stops going to class. He opens a French course out of spite for a YouTube comment and finishes the first month of lessons in an afternoon. By the third night he’s dreaming in liaisons and nasal vowels. He writes a note to himself in a margin: "This is unhealthy. Also holy shit." Victoria texts at 2 a.m., "you up?" He answers with a paragraph in French just to amuse himself. She replies "wtf" and an emoji he can’t interpret.

The obsession feels like clarity. He decides to "finally grok general relativity." Minkowski diagrams spill over the walls on taped printer paper. He wakes to scribbles he doesn’t remember making: tensor notation in marker on the bathroom mirror, post-its that say "time is geometry you idiot." He leaves unfinished freelance gigs to rot, misses Ravi's birthday, ignores Mira’s voice notes. Messages stack unread. Each time a pang of guilt touches him, it dissolves under the weight of another problem: "derive Schwarzschild radius from first principles before sleep." Sleep becomes optional. He doesn't feel tired; he feels propelled.

Weeks blur. His fridge empties except for pickles and cold brew. He eats microwaved rice standing up. Victoria drifts in and out—sometimes to hook up, sometimes to judge him. "You look like a ghost," she says, pulling at the collar of his stained shirt. He laughs, says "je suis obsédé," and she rolls her eyes. She doesn’t see the units under the desk; Hugo barely sees them himself. He only feels the pull: finish the proof, memorize another conjugation, chase the next article.

The notice on his door is the first thing that breaks the trance. Eviction. He hasn’t paid rent in two months because he forgot rent exists. The landlord bangs, curses; Hugo promises to be out by morning. He packs books, laptop, clothes. When he reaches behind the desk, his hand hits the cold metal of the units. For a second he doesn’t recognize them. Then the hum in his head sharpens with awareness—oh. He’s been plugged in.

He powers them down one by one. The silence lands like air after a long dive, lungs aching as they fill. His shoulders drop. The apartment sounds return: distant traffic, a leaking faucet. Under the relief there’s a hollow ache, like being left behind by a friend who never promised to stay. He sits on the floor surrounded by boxes, the dead units in his lap, and realizes the relentless drive wasn’t entirely his. He feels cheated and grateful in the same breath. Outside, someone honks. Inside, Hugo just breathes.
